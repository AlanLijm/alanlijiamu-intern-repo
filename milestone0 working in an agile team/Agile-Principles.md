# 0.12 Agile Principles & Frameworks
Research & Learn
What is Agile, and how does it differ from traditional project management?

Agile is an iterative approach to building software: work is broken into small increments, delivered frequently, and adjusted based on continuous feedback. Traditional project management (often called "Waterfall") is sequential — requirements are fixed upfront, then design, build, test, and release happen in that fixed order, with little room to change course once a phase is complete.

The key difference is how each handles change and feedback:

Traditional/Waterfall: plan everything upfront, follow the plan, change is expensive and disruptive once underway.
Agile: plan just enough to start, deliver small pieces frequently, and use real feedback to adapt the plan as you go.
What are the core values and principles of Agile?

The Agile Manifesto defines four core values:

Individuals and interactions over processes and tools
Working software over comprehensive documentation
Customer collaboration over contract negotiation
Responding to change over following a plan

Importantly, the items on the right still matter — Agile just values the items on the left more. The Manifesto is backed by 12 principles, with recurring themes including: delivering value early and continuously, welcoming changing requirements (even late in development), close daily collaboration between business and developers, sustainable pace, and regular reflection on how to improve as a team.

What are the differences between Scrum and Kanban, and when is each used?
Scrum works in fixed-length iterations (sprints, e.g. 2 weeks), with defined roles (Product Owner, Scrum Master, Dev Team) and a set of ceremonies (sprint planning, daily stand-up, review, retro). It suits work where scope can be reasonably planned a couple of weeks ahead and the team benefits from a regular rhythm of commitment and review.
Kanban is continuous flow: work items move through columns on a board as capacity allows, with a focus on limiting work in progress (WIP) rather than fixed-length iterations. It suits work with unpredictable or frequently shifting priorities, support/maintenance-heavy work, or teams that want flexibility to reprioritize at any time rather than being locked into a sprint commitment.
Why does Focus Bear lean towards Kanban instead of Scrum?

I think Focus Bear leans towards Kanban because it's a small team where fixed sprint ceremonies would add more overhead than value. Priorities also shift frequently as the product evolves, so a preference for continuous delivery over batching work into fixed iterations fits the team better.

Reflection

What do you think are the biggest benefits and challenges of Agile?

I think the biggest challenge is that outcomes and timelines are hard to guarantee, because Agile is a continuously changing process where requirements can shift at any point. The biggest benefit is that it's an iterative process, which improves the flexibility of the project — the team can adapt quickly instead of being locked into a rigid plan that no longer fits reality.

How can Agile principles help improve your role and responsibilities?

As a backend developer, Agile principles like welcoming changing requirements give me more flexibility — I can adjust my code based on feedback or new information I encounter along the way, instead of being locked into an initial approach. This also gives me more room to experiment and test different solutions, which helps me arrive at better implementations rather than settling on the first idea that works. Additionally, the principle of valuing working software over comprehensive documentation lets me focus on delivering functional, reviewable code quickly, rather than spending excessive time on upfront documentation before anything is built.

Scrum vs. Kanban, in my own words: 
Scrum works well for planned, fixed-scope work — it uses fixed-length sprints and clearly defined roles, which gives the team a predictable rhythm to plan around. However, its shortage is flexibility: once a sprint starts, changes are discouraged. Kanban, on the other hand, has high flexibility since work flows continuously and priorities can shift at any time, but this makes outcomes and timelines harder to predict compared to Scrum's fixed sprint commitments.
