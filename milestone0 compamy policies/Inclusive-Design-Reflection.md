# 0.4 Inclusive Design Reflection — Working with Vulnerable Populations
Research & Learn

Who are considered vulnerable populations, and what challenges might they face in digital spaces? Vulnerable populations in a digital-product context include neurodivergent people (ADHD, Autism, dyslexia), people with mental health conditions, older adults, people with physical or sensory disabilities, and people in crisis or under high stress. In digital spaces they commonly face cognitive overload from cluttered interfaces, inconsistent or unpredictable navigation, sensory triggers (autoplay video, harsh colours, sudden sounds), and shame-inducing design patterns (streak-breaking guilt, aggressive notifications) that punish rather than support inconsistency.

What ethical considerations are important when designing or working with neurodivergent individuals? The core principle is designing with rather than for — treating neurodivergent traits as a difference in wiring, not a deficit to be fixed. Practically this means avoiding overwhelming UX (too many choices or alerts at once), respecting sensory needs (allowing users to reduce animation, sound, and visual noise), giving predictable, low-surprise interaction patterns, and never shaming a user for missed streaks or "failure" to be productive — executive dysfunction is not a motivation problem.

How can you make interactions and content more accessible for people with ADHD or Autism?

Use simple, literal, unambiguous language — avoid idioms or sarcasm that can be misread.
Keep navigation predictable: consistent layout, minimal menu depth, clear labelling.
Reduce cognitive load: one primary action per screen, progressive disclosure instead of dumping all options at once.
Offer customisable sensory settings (reduced motion, muted colour themes, adjustable notification frequency).
Break tasks into small, concrete steps rather than open-ended instructions.

How can we support neurodivergent team members in a professional setting?

Put expectations and instructions in writing so nothing depends on remembering a verbal conversation.
Be explicit and literal in communication rather than relying on implied meaning or tone.
Allow flexible working hours/environments and don't treat a different working rhythm as a lack of commitment.
Ask people directly what support or accommodations help them, rather than assuming.
Reflection

How can you adjust your communication style to be more inclusive of neurodivergent users and teammates? When talking to a neurodivergent user, I would use direct, clear language instead of implying things or expecting them to "read between the lines." I would avoid packing too much information into one message, and check in to make sure they've understood before moving on. For anything important, I would like to double-check it explicitly rather than assuming it landed. I would also avoid sarcasm or joking in ways that could be misread literally.

What are some common UX or communication pitfalls that might make Focus Bear less accessible or supportive? One common pitfall could be showing too many settings or messages at once during onboarding, which can overwhelm users who already struggle with focus. Another is using a "streak" system that makes users feel like they failed if they miss a day — this could discourage them instead of motivating them.

What is one practical change you can make in your work to better support vulnerable populations? One practical change I can make is to review any feature I build against a simple checklist: is there one clear action per screen, is the language simple and literal, and can the user reduce notifications or animations if needed.

Task

First-person account. I read a first-person account (from an ADHD community discussion) describing a recurring cycle of downloading a productivity app, over-engineering it, then abandoning it — repeated for years. The person explained that the problem wasn't the apps themselves, but that any system with too much structure or friction couldn't survive their ADHD. What finally worked was the opposite of a "perfect system": using the simplest possible tools (a plain notes app with zero setup and no overdue/guilt indicators, plus a voice-capture app to turn scattered thoughts into tasks without needing to sit down and plan). Their key insight was that a tool only survives long-term if it works on your worst ADHD day with zero resistance — low effort beats powerful features, and any UI that punishes inconsistency (like overdue counters) kills consistency rather than encouraging it.

One design or communication improvement for Focus Bear. One improvement I'd suggest for Focus Bear is removing punitive elements from the streak/habit tracking — like a strict overdue counter or a full streak reset after one missed day. Instead, missing a day could be treated as normal rather than a failure, with a low-pressure way to pick back up (e.g. "restart with one small thing" instead of "you broke your streak"). This directly reflects what the person in the account I read described: any system with guilt-based mechanics didn't survive their ADHD, while the tools that stuck were the ones with zero punishment for inconsistency.

Practice response to a hypothetical user struggling with executive functioning:

Hi, thanks for telling me — there's nothing wrong with feeling stuck like this, it happens to a lot of people. Let's not worry about the whole task right now. Is there one small part we could start with, even just two minutes? We can go at whatever pace feels okay today, no pressure.
